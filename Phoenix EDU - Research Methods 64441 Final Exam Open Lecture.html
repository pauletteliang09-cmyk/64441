Qualitative vs Quantitative

QualitativeQuantitative
Main purposeExplore meanings, experiences, “how” and “why”Measure, describe patterns, test relationships (“how many / how much”)
DataWords, images, storiesNumbers
SampleSmall, chosen on purpose (purposive, snowball)Large, ideally probability sampling (to generalise)
MethodsSemi-structured interviews, focus groups, observationQuestionnaires (Likert scales), experiments
AnalysisThematic analysis, content analysisDescriptive stats, t-test / ANOVA, correlation, regression, factor analysis
QualityAuthenticity, plausibility, criticalityReliability & validity
Strength / limit✓ Rich depth   ✕ Hard to generalise✓ Generalisable, comparable   ✕ Less depth on “why”

EXAMPLE · Q7: Recommend one sampling technique for an online-shopping satisfaction study (mid-market fashion brand)

PI recommend stratified random sampling.

EThe population is split into groups (strata), and people are chosen at random from each group.

AThe brand can use its online customer database as the sampling frame and create strata by age group or purchase frequency.

JThis is appropriate because every type of customer is represented, so the satisfaction results can be generalised to all online shoppers. A limitation is that it needs an accurate customer list.

Example 1 · Step 1

Decode the Question

Practice Question 4 · 25 marks Discuss at least two ethical issues that can arise when collecting data using online questionnaires. Explain how a researcher can address these issues to ensure their study is conducted ethically.
What this means
  • Discuss = explain each issue in detail, not just list it.
  • At least two = two good issues are enough. Depth beats a long list.
  • Online = show why the risk is different online.
  • Two tasks = issue + solution. Missing solutions = lost marks.
Lecture link: Week 2 · Research Ethics: Key Principles
5-minute answer plan
  1. Intro: online surveys are fast and cheap, but there is no face-to-face contact
  2. Issue 1 Informed consent → solution
  3. Issue 2 Confidentiality & anonymity → solution
  4. (Bonus) Issue 3 Voluntary participation → solution
  5. Close: ethics approval before collecting data

02 · Example 1 · Topic A: Ethics
PHOENIX EDU
菲尼克斯教育
Example 1 · Step 3

Model Answer & Highlights

Online questionnaires are fast and cheap, but the researcher never meets the participants, so some ethical risks become bigger online. Two key issues are informed consent and confidentiality.

Issue 1Informed consent. People must understand the purpose, tasks, data use and risks before they agree. Online, many people click “Next” without reading, and under-18s may take part. The first page should be a short participant information sheet, followed by a required consent box and an age-screening question.

Issue 2Confidentiality and anonymity. Survey tools can record IP addresses or emails, and online data can be leaked. The researcher should collect only necessary data, switch off IP tracking, remove identifiers, use a secure university platform and report only group results, following GDPR and the UK Data Protection Act 2018.

Issue 3Voluntary participation. Forced answers or large prizes can create pressure, so questions should be optional and people should know they can stop at any time.

Finally, the student must check the study with the University Ethics Decision Tool and their supervisor before collecting data.

Why this scores well
  • Uses lecture terms (informed consent, anonymity, GDPR)
  • Every issue is linked to the online context
  • Every issue has a practical solution
  • Clear signposting: “Issue 1… Issue 2…”
Common mistakes
  • Listing all 6 principles with no explanation
  • A general answer that could be about interviews
  • Problems with no solutions (only half the question)

Example 2 · Step 1

Decode the Question

Practice Question 11 · 25 marks You have two focus group transcripts with customers discussing their experiences at a recent pop-up event run by a fashion brand. Outline the key steps you would follow to analyse these data using thematic analysis.
What this means
  • Outline = give the main steps in order, briefly. No long theory.
  • Thematic analysis is given: do not choose another method.
  • Two focus groups = compare the groups and notice how people interact.
  • Pop-up event = give an example from this event in each step.
Lecture link: Week 7 · Qualitative Analysis
5-minute answer plan
  1. One line: what thematic analysis is (Braun & Clarke, 2006)
  2. Steps 1–6, each with action + pop-up example
  3. One line on focus groups: compare the 2 groups
  4. Close: themes answer the research question
Don’t confuse: content analysis = count categories (Q12). Thematic analysis = interpret meanings (Q11, Q13).

02 · Example 2 · Topic B: Qualitative analysis
PHOENIX EDU
菲尼克斯教育
Example 2 · Step 3

Model Answer & Highlights

Thematic analysis finds patterns of meaning (themes) in qualitative data. I would follow Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six steps.

1Familiarise: read both transcripts several times and note first ideas, e.g. many people mention the queue.

2Code: label useful pieces of text, e.g. “long waiting time”, “limited edition”, “photo wall”.

3Search for themes: group similar codes, e.g. “limited edition” + “staff told the designer’s story” → Feeling exclusive.

4Review: go back to the data to check that each theme fits both focus groups; merge or split themes.

5Define & name: write a clear definition for each theme, e.g. Social media value: the event is enjoyed as content to share.

6Report: present each theme with short quotes and link it to the research question and the brand’s next pop-up.

Because these are focus groups, I would also note how participants agree or disagree, as group interaction is part of the data.

Why this scores well
  • Steps named and in order (key author cited)
  • Each step = action + pop-up example
  • Uses the focus-group feature: comparison & interaction
  • Shows a theme is a shared meaning, not just a topic
Common mistakes
  • Counting words (that is content analysis)
  • Only listing the 6 step names
  • Skipping “review”: not checking themes against the data

Example 3 · Step 1

Decode the Question

Practice Question 20 · 25 marks A study of Gen Z consumers runs a regression analysis with concern about environmental impact (predictor) and monthly spending on ultra-fast fashion (outcome) and finds a negative regression coefficient (b = –5.20, p < 0.01, R² = 0.20). Briefly explain what this result means in plain language.
What this means
  • Briefly = short and focused. No calculations needed.
  • Plain language = explain it so a brand manager can understand. Translate every number.
  • There are 3 numbers → explain all 3, plus the direction.
Lecture link: Week 8 · Quantitative Analysis
5-sentence answer plan
  1. Direction: negative → more concern, less spending
  2. b: size of the change
  3. p: is it likely to be chance?
  4. R²: how much is explained?
  5. Caution: relationship, not proof of cause

Example 3 · Step 2

Illustration: Reading the 3 Numbers

Concern score: 4 / 7
Predicted spend: £44.40
Illustrative data. Only b = –5.20 comes from the question; the starting point (£65.20) and the 1–7 scale are examples.
b = –5.20
Direction + size. Each 1-point rise in concern → about £5.20 less spent per month, on average.
p < 0.01
Significant. Less than a 1% chance of a result like this if there were really no relationship → very unlikely to be luck.
R² = 0.20. Concern explains 20% of the differences in spending. 80% is due to other things: price, income, trends, social media…
⚠ Correlation ≠ causation. A survey shows a relationship. It does not prove that concern causes lower spending.

02 · Example 3 · Topic C: Quantitative analysis
PHOENIX EDU
菲尼克斯教育
Example 3 · Step 3

Model Answer & Highlights

DirectionGen Z consumers who are more worried about the environment tend to spend less on ultra-fast fashion each month.

bFor every 1-point increase in concern, monthly spending is about £5.20 lower on average.

pThe result is statistically significant (p < 0.01), so it is very unlikely to be due to chance.

R²However, concern explains only 20% of the differences in spending. The other 80% comes from other factors, such as price, income or trends.

CautionThis shows a relationship, not cause and effect. Brands should not assume that raising concern alone will reduce spending.

Why this scores well
  • Every number is translated into everyday words
  • Gives direction, size, significance and strength
  • Adds a critical point: not causation, 80% unexplained
  • Short, as “briefly” asks
Common mistakes
  • “R² = 0.20 means 20% of people…” ✕
  • “p < 0.01 proves the hypothesis is true” ✕
  • “Concern causes people to spend less” ✕
  • Using jargon when asked for plain language

Your Turn: 2-Minute Plans

2:00
Q7 · Topic A
A student wants to research customers’ satisfaction with online shopping for a mid-market fashion brand. Recommend one sampling technique and briefly justify why it is appropriate.
Recommend: stratified random sampling from the brand’s online customer list.
Why: random selection from each customer group (e.g. age, purchase frequency) → representative → results can be generalised.
Limit: needs an accurate sampling frame. (Convenience sampling is quicker but less representative.)
Q17 · Topic C
A brand wants to know if average purchase intention (1–7 scale) differs between customers who saw three different Instagram adverts. Name one suitable analysis and explain why.
Name: one-way ANOVA.
Why: it compares the means of 3 or more groups. The outcome (1–7 scale) is numeric and the groups are separate.
Extra: if significant, post-hoc tests show which adverts differ. A t-test only compares 2 groups.

Conclusion

Dos & Don’ts

✓ DO
  • Underline the command word and answer exactly that
  • Apply every point to the scenario (brand, Gen Z, event…)
  • Use correct lecture terms, then explain them simply
  • Justify with “because…”; add a strength or limitation
  • Write in short paragraphs with signposts (First, Second…)
  • Keep to 30 minutes per question
  • Revise all three topics: you need four answers
✕ DON’T
  • Don’t write everything you know about a topic
  • Don’t memorise model answers word for word
  • Don’t leave a question blank: each one is 25%
  • Don’t mix up: probability vs non-probability, reliability vs validity, thematic vs content analysis
  • Don’t say a survey or regression proves cause
  • Don’t skip statistics: it is the biggest question group
  • Don’t forget your phone (Duo Mobile) and student ID

Frequently Asked Questions

1Can I use my notes or Canvas?+
No. It is closed book. The Assessment Desktop blocks the internet and Canvas unit content; only the exam page is open.
2Can I use AI tools?+
No. The exam is AI Category A: AI prohibited.
3Will the practice questions be on the exam?+
Not the same ones. They show the style and level. Revise the full lecture material and practise applying it to new situations.
4Is the literature review tested?+
It is not one of the directly examinable topics (design & ethics, qualitative, quantitative). It was assessed in your coursework.
5Do I need to calculate statistics?+
The practice questions ask you to choose the right analysis and interpret results (e.g. b, p, R²), not to calculate by hand.
6How long should each answer be?+
No word limit is given. Aim for a focused answer you can write in about 22 minutes. Quality and application matter more than length.
7Do I need references?+
Not like coursework. Naming a key author (e.g. Braun & Clarke for thematic analysis) can show knowledge, but correct, applied content matters most.
8Can I write in bullet points?+
Use short paragraphs (the unit’s advice). Numbered steps are fine for “outline” questions, but each step still needs a full sentence.
9When do I get my result?+
Summative feedback is planned for Monday 8 February 2027.
10What if I am ill on exam day?+
Apply for mitigating circumstances and contact your personal tutor and the Student Support & Wellbeing team as soon as possible.
